In short

On May 7, 2026, Berkeley’s City Council voted 8–1 against executing Flock Safety’s full Master Services Agreement, and 5–4 to extend the existing 52-camera license plate reader contract for twelve months. The successor procurement was referred to the City Manager as an RFP. The next mandatory return to Council is an efficacy assessment due roughly June 2027 — to the Council seated December 1, 2026.

This page sets out what the record shows about that contract: what Council restricted, what the restrictions reach, what the audits measure, what the technology has been credited with, and what it costs. It is built on 120 sources, 89 of which currently have an archived copy.

Questions

What did Council actually decide on May 7?

Two separate things, and they are often merged. On May 7, 2026, Council voted 8–1 not to execute the full Flock Master Services Agreement — the fixed cameras, the community video streams, the first-responder drones, and the investigations software. 23 Eight of nine members voted that way, including the four councilmembers who, as The Berkeley Scanner reported, had co-signed an op-ed supporting renewal and expansion two days earlier. 23 71

That vote is not in dispute here, and the record of it belongs in any honest account of what Berkeley did.

The extension was a different vote. Council severed it, and it carried 5–4 — twelve months of the existing 52-camera ALPR contract for up to $200,000, with the successor procurement referred to the City Manager for an RFP. 23 23 Four members voted no: Bartlett, Tregub, Lunaparra, Ishii.

So the question of whether Berkeley keeps buying this product from this company was not settled in May. It was deferred by twelve months, on a one-vote margin.

What does BPD's audit measure, and what does it not?

It reports 100 percent on BPD authorized access, 100 percent on BPD permitted uses, 100 percent on external authorized access, and 99.81 percent on external permitted uses. 32 The FY2025 annual report states that every audit required by policy was completed, and that Internal Affairs received no external complaints about these technologies. 32

Those numbers are real and the page does not dispute them.

What they measure is whether people with authorized access followed the rules when they ran searches. That is worth measuring. It is not the same as measuring whether the data stayed where Berkeley put it, and two failures in the last year turned on the difference.

In February 2026, Berkeley's own Police Accountability Board reported that Mountain View police had discovered Flock silently enabled a nationwide access setting on their system, allowing federal agencies including ATF, the Air Force, and the GSA Inspector General to reach local ALPR data — along with a statewide lookup feature left on for 29 of 30 cameras, producing roughly 600,000 unauthorized searches by more than 250 California agencies over more than a year. The chief shut down all thirty cameras on February 2. The council terminated the contract unanimously on February 24. 8

No local compliance rule would have caught that, because nobody local did anything wrong. The setting changed on the vendor's side.

In June 2026, the San Francisco Police Department released an audit finding that its Flock data had been improperly searched nearly 300 times over about a year — 299 inquiries, or roughly 0.005 percent of queries, as The San Francisco Standard reported. 118 SFPD had lawfully authorized the Northern California Regional Intelligence Center to reach its data. KQED reported that NCRIC then extended access to analysts at the Western States Information Network without SFPD's knowledge, and those analysts searched on behalf of outside agencies. 119118

The first hop was authorized. The violation happened at the second, where a rule written about who may have access does not reach. It took about a year and an audit to surface.

Berkeley's restrictions are written at the first hop.

What has the ALPR system been credited with?

The City reports that during 2025 fixed ALPRs were instrumental in at least 55 arrests, assisted in more than 100 other investigations, and led to the recovery of at least 43 stolen vehicles, and that robbery closure rates rose ten percentage points in the nine months after implementation. 6

That is the City's own accounting, and there is no comparison group in it. A closure rate that rises over nine months rose during nine months in which other things also happened.

The strongest published evidence that ALPR expansion helps is a 2025 evaluation of the Atlantic City Police Department in the Justice Evaluation Journal. It found the expansion did not reduce violent crime, and was associated with reductions in shootings, motor vehicle thefts, and property crime. 109116

Three things belong next to that finding. The department sits on a barrier island, and the cameras were installed on all of its entrances and exits, scanning every vehicle entering or leaving. 116 One of the two authors is James A. Sarkos, chief of the department being evaluated. 117 And the same evaluation found the technology's use skewed toward particular investigative units rather than patrol. 116

Berkeley has no perimeter to seal. The finding does not transfer.

Does ending the contract matter if other cities keep theirs?

El Cerrito declined to renew on May 6, 2026; Contra Costa News reported its cameras went dark June 7 and removal began in July. 40 The Los Angeles Police Department let its three-year agreement expire, with CIO Dean Gialamas telling TechCrunch the department had serious concerns around civil liberties and privacy. 56 UW–Madison police did not renew. 60

Other places went the other way, and the page is not going to pretend otherwise. Cleveland 19 News reported that a council committee voted to end the city's contract in June, then approved a six-month extension about three weeks later. 77 76 Voice of OC reported Costa Mesa voting to keep its contract through April 2027. 61 Capitol City Now reported Springfield, Illinois approving a two-year contract. 39

The premise is also worth examining. What Berkeley controls is Berkeley's data — 52 cameras recording the vehicles of people who live here, held in a vendor's system and shared onward under rules whose failure modes are documented above. A neighbor's cameras are not Berkeley's to cancel and were never the question.

Did federal immigration agencies search Berkeley's data?

The audit states that no federal or out-of-state agencies searched BPD's ALPR network. 32 It records five searches containing the string DHS and attributes them to the Desert Hot Springs Police Department. 32

Both of those are correct, and any claim that federal immigration agents searched Berkeley's cameras is not supported by this record. The page does not make one.

What the record does show is narrower and still worth stating. In July 2025 BPD identified noncompliant use through the statewide ALPR lookup function: external agencies entered ICE once and CBP twice in the reason field while searching statewide data that included Berkeley's. 33 BPD disabled the statewide lookup tool. 32 By order of the chief, network access was revoked for all agencies outside the nine Bay Area counties plus Sacramento County, and the audit reports zero searches referencing a federal immigration agency since. 32

Berkeley found a problem and closed it. That is the strongest fact available to the other side of this argument, and it is in the record because it happened.

It also establishes the point. The exposure was real enough to act on, it came through a sharing feature rather than a rule violation, and it was found after the fact.

Is the 2027 efficacy review the right venue for this decision?

A real one would be progress, and the page says so plainly.

The review already exists on paper. A Lunaparra amendment adopted as part of the May 7 motion requires that the extension include an efficacy assessment and a compliance review, presented to Council at least thirty days before the extension ends. 23 With the extension running roughly twelve months from July 2026, that lands around June 2027. 23

Three things about that timing. The council that receives it is the one seated December 1, 2026, not the one that ordered it. No ALPR item appeared on any Council agenda between May 7 and August 4, 2026. 23 And under BMC 2.99.030 the City Manager controls whether and when a surveillance acquisition report comes forward, so the agenda timing on this originates with staff rather than with Council. 23

A review that arrives thirty days before a contract lapses, to a body that did not write its terms, is not a decision point. It is a notification.

What would replace the contract?

The procurement that would replace it already exists on paper.

The twelve-month extension runs out in 2027. Berkeley does not have to renew it, and the procurement that would replace it has already been referred to the City Manager as an RFP in consultation with the Police Accountability Board and BPD. 23 That process can proceed without this vendor.

If Council will not do that, the efficacy review has to be worth the name. That means publishing the audits rather than summarizing them, identifying the agencies involved in each finding rather than declining to, and accounting for the searches BPD itself runs against other jurisdictions' data — which no published audit currently covers. It means the review arriving early enough to be acted on rather than thirty days before expiry. Council already directed staff to explore contract terms carrying a $290,000 penalty per violation 23 and to amend BMC 2.99 to add a violation and termination clause for surveillance vendors. 23 Those referrals should produce something before the next contract is signed, not after.

That is a smaller ask than ending the contract. It would still be progress, and the campaign would treat it as such.
